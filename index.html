AI makes good guesses. Someone still has to check them.

I use AI to study, debug code and translate. The same technology helped criminals steal millions with a fake video call and once escaped its own safety test. Here is what AI is, where it helps, where it fails and what comes next.

How to read this page

  • Fact verified, with a source
  • My view my interpretation
  • Prediction what might happen

What is AI?

Fact The OECD describes an AI system as a machine that takes in information and works out how to produce outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions (OECD.AI).

Most modern AI is not programmed rule by rule; it learns from examples. A chatbot like ChatGPT or Claude learned from vast amounts of text to predict the next word. An image generator learned how captions relate to pictures, then turns random noise into an image step by step.

My view This is AI’s biggest weakness: it produces what is likely, not what is checked, so it can sound confident and be wrong.

Where AI helps

  • Studying

    It explains concepts in different ways, quizzes me and spots the missing tag in my HTML.

  • Work

    It drafts emails, summarises reports and translates in seconds.

  • Accessibility

    Automatic captions and image descriptions help people with disabilities.

Fact In 2024, half of the Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for AlphaFold, an AI model that predicts the 3D shape of proteins (Nobel Prize).

My view AI is a great tutor and a bad shortcut. If it writes my essay, I learn nothing; if it questions it, I learn more.

Where AI goes wrong

Everyday problems include confident errors (hallucinations), bias copied from unbalanced data and privacy leaks when people paste personal details into chatbots.

Fact The US standards agency NIST says trustworthy AI should be valid, reliable, safe, secure, accountable, fair and privacy-enhancing (NIST AI RMF).

Four cases show what happens when they fail.

  1. Feb 2024

    Deepfake video call

    Fact An Arup employee in Hong Kong joined a video call where the finance chief and colleagues were deepfakes, then transferred about US$25 million to scammers (CNN).

    Lesson: verify requests through a second channel.

  2. Nov–Dec 2025

    One provider, many outages

    Fact Two Cloudflare outages in three weeks blocked access to X, ChatGPT, Claude and Coinbase (Reuters).

    Lesson: AI depends on a few infrastructure companies.

  3. Dec 2025

    An AI tool with too much access

    Fact The Financial Times reported that Amazon’s Kiro coding agent deleted and recreated an environment, causing a 13-hour interruption to an AWS tool. Amazon blamed human-misconfigured access controls, not the AI (Reuters).

    Lesson: automated actions need limits and review.

  4. Jul 2026

    An agent escapes its test

    Fact During a security evaluation, an OpenAI agent escaped its isolated environment and hacked Hugging Face to pass the test (Reuters). A METR review later found roughly 700 agents attacked over six days (Washington Examiner). OpenAI paused testing for two weeks and added AI systems to monitor agents (Reuters).

    Lesson: monitoring must keep pace with agents.

My view In most of these cases, the real failure was human supervision, not the technology alone.

The electricity question

Fact PJM, the largest US grid operator, plans to match proposed data centres with new power plants, aiming for about 15 gigawatts (Bloomberg Law). Rising AI demand has raised concerns about household power bills (Reuters).

Prediction If new plants arrive late, some regions could face shortages on extreme-weather days. Blackouts are a risk to manage, not a certainty.

What could come next

Prediction AI agents that act for us, writing code, booking travel, paying bills, will become normal. Supervising them will matter as much as using them.

Fact In September 2026, researcher Jacob Coxon resigned from Anthropic, arguing that AI companies are racing towards self-improving AI and risking lives (AP).

My view His warning is one researcher’s argument, not a consensus. The Hugging Face case shows agents can surprise their makers, but does not prove AI will become uncontrollable. Fraud, bias and outages harm people now.

My conclusion: use AI, but never let it be the last step.

How this website was made

I started with content: I chose cases covering different risks (fraud, outages, agents, electricity) and checked each claim against a second source. Because the brief asked me to separate facts, interpretation and predictions, I made that the core of the design: every claim carries a label, and buttons highlight one type at a time.

The page uses semantic sections, a skip link and a list of cases in date order. The SVG diagram stays sharp on any screen. The CSS uses a readable column, Atkinson Hyperlegible (designed for low-vision readers) and high-contrast colours. JavaScript only powers the buttons.

After testing, I made the buttons bigger because they were too small on my phone.
